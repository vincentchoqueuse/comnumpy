Raman amplification#

Distributed Raman amplification: gain spectra and the pump equations.

Two objects and one solver, split the way the physics is:

  • RamanGainSpectrum – the shape of the Raman gain against the pump-signal frequency shift, normalized to 1 at its peak, with its provenance. It is a property of the glass, so it is catalogued the way D15/D20/D43 catalogue the frequency and delay axes: frozen value object, registry, and a self-check at construction against the figures its source publishes.

  • the peak coefficient \(g_R / A_\mathrm{eff}\) is not in that object. It is a property of the fibre – SMF, DCF and NZDSF differ by a large factor through the effective area and the germanium doping – so it is an argument of the solver, next to the losses.

  • solve_raman() – the coupled power equations, integrated forwards when only the co-propagating pump is on and solved as a two-point boundary value problem otherwise.

There is no Processor here, on purpose. Raman lives in the power domain and the split-step propagation of FiberLink lives in the field domain: a block that multiplied the field by a lumped gain at the end of a span would describe a discrete amplifier, which is the one thing distributed amplification is not. What this module produces is the gain profile \(G(z)\), meant to be applied inside the linear step.

class RamanGainSpectrum(*, lorentzian: tuple[float, float] | None = None, triangular: float | None = None, tabulated: tuple[ndarray, ndarray] | None = None, quoted_at: tuple[float, float, float] | None = None, standard: str = 'custom', reference: str = '')[source]#

Normalized Raman gain shape against the pump-signal frequency shift.

Signal Model#

Stimulated Raman scattering transfers power from a pump at \(\nu_p\) to a signal at \(\nu_s < \nu_p\), with an efficiency that depends only on the Stokes shift

\[\Delta \nu = \nu_p - \nu_s\]

This object carries the shape \(\tilde{g}(\Delta \nu)\), normalized so that \(\max_{\Delta \nu} \tilde{g} = 1\); the fibre’s peak coefficient \(g_R / A_\mathrm{eff}\) multiplies it (see solve_raman()). Exactly one of the two closed forms below is selected, the D41 way – one keyword per parameterization, no discriminator argument.

Damped-oscillator model (lorentzian). The Raman response of silica is modelled by a single damped oscillator of period \(\tau_1\) and lifetime \(\tau_2\), whose Fourier transform is

\[\tilde{H}_R(\omega) = \frac{\tau_1^{-2} + \tau_2^{-2}} {\tau_1^{-2} - \left(\omega + i \tau_2^{-1}\right)^2}, \qquad \omega = 2 \pi \Delta \nu\]

and the gain is \(\operatorname{Im} \tilde{H}_R\), normalized here to its peak. \(\tilde{H}_R(0) = 1\) fixes the normalization of the response itself.

Triangular model (triangular). Over the first few terahertz the gain is linear in the shift, which is what the closed-form inter-channel Raman tilt of a WDM comb relies on:

\[\begin{split}\tilde{g}(\Delta \nu) = \begin{cases} \Delta \nu / \Delta \nu_\mathrm{peak} & 0 \leq \Delta \nu \leq \Delta \nu_\mathrm{peak}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\end{split}\]

Normalizing to the peak leaves a single free number, \(\Delta \nu_\mathrm{peak}\): the slope is then \(1/\Delta \nu_\mathrm{peak}\) in normalized units and the absolute slope comes from the fibre’s peak coefficient.

\((\tau_1, \tau_2)\) in femtoseconds. Mutually exclusive with triangular.

pair_scaling(frequency_Hz: ndarray) → ndarray[source]#

Waveguide correction of the gain, per (receiver, partner) pair.

The gain shape() is a property of the glass and depends on the Stokes shift alone. The coefficient that multiplies \(P_i P_j\) in a power equation is not that shape but \(g_R / A_{\mathrm{eff}}\), and the effective area is a property of the waveguide: the mode spreads as the wavelength grows, so the same Stokes shift buys less gain further into the infrared. With a Gaussian mode this reduces to a one-parameter law, \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}(\nu) = A_0 k / (\ln(\nu/\nu_0) + k)\) with \(k = \pi a^2 / A_0\). A pair is charged the mean of the two areas, and the gain is referred back to the frequency the table was quoted at.

Across one band this is a few per cent; across C+L+S it is the difference between a flat prediction and a wrong tilt.

Axes: returns a matrix (n, n), one factor per ordered pair, with j the partner that feeds i.

Parameters:

frequency_Hz (array_like) – Frequencies of the waves, in Hz.

Returns:

np.ndarray – Scaling factors, all 1 when the spectrum does not record where it was quoted.

References

The Gaussian-mode form \(w = a/\sqrt{\ln V}\) and the arithmetic-mean overlap are transcribed from GNPy (gnpy/core/parameters.py, effective_area_scaling and effective_area_overlap, BSD-3-Clause), which does not give a source for them; the one-parameter law above is that model with the index contrast eliminated, and is derived in validation/optical_raman_gnpy.py rather than taken from a paper. The gain profile it is applied to comes from A. D’Amico et al., J. Lightwave Technol., vol. 40, pp. 3499-3511, 2022, Section III.D.

The confirmation that matters is empirical rather than bibliographic: with this law the coupling coefficients match GNPy’s element by element on the gain side, ratio 1.0000.

Examples

>>> spectrum = get_gain_spectrum("blow-wood")
>>> float(np.max(np.abs(spectrum.pair_scaling(
...     np.array([193e12, 206e12])) - 1.0)))
0.0

class RamanSolution(z_km: ndarray, signal_W: ndarray, pump_forward_W: ndarray, pump_backward_W: ndarray, ase_W: ndarray, loss_only_W: ndarray, bandwidth_Hz: float, frequency_signal_Hz: float | ndarray, _pump_transmission: float | ndarray = 1.0)[source]#

Power profiles along the span, and what is read off them.

Signal Model#

All profiles are functions of the distance \(z\) from the span input. Per signal \(i\), the figures of merit are

\[G_\mathrm{on\text{-}off} = \frac{P_{s,i}(L)}{P_{s,i}(L)\big|_{P_p = 0}} = \frac{P_{s,i}(L)}{P_{s,i}(0) e^{-\alpha_{s,i} L}}, \qquad G_\mathrm{net} = \frac{P_{s,i}(L)}{P_{s,i}(0)}\]

– the on-off gain is what the pumps buy, the net gain is what comes out of the span – and the effective noise figure

\[F_\mathrm{eff} = \frac{1}{G_\mathrm{net}} \left(1 + \frac{P_\mathrm{ASE}(L)}{h \nu_s B}\right)\]

which is effective because it refers the noise of the whole distributed span to a hypothetical discrete amplifier; it may be below 0 dB, and that is the reason distributed amplification is used at all.

Shapes. Each profile carries one row per wave, (n, n_z), except when its group holds a single wave: a solve with one signal and one pump wavelength returns the plain (n_z,) curves, so the single-channel case reads exactly as it did before the multi-wave generalization. The figures of merit follow the same rule: a float for one signal, an array of one value per signal otherwise.

z_km#

Distance grid, in km, from 0 to the span length.

Type:

np.ndarray

signal_W#

Signal powers \(P_{s,i}(z)\) in W, one row per signal.

Type:

np.ndarray

pump_forward_W, pump_backward_W

Pump powers \(P_p^{+}(z)\) and \(P_p^{-}(z)\) in W, one row per pump wavelength. Both are given on the same \(z\) grid; the backward pumps are launched at \(z = L\). A pump that is off in one direction is stored as a zero row, so the two arrays always have the same shape.

Type:

np.ndarray

ase_W#

Amplified spontaneous emission \(P_\mathrm{ASE}(z)\) in W, one row per signal, in the reference bandwidth of the solve.

Type:

np.ndarray

bandwidth_Hz#

Reference bandwidth \(B\) the ASE was integrated over.

Type:

float

frequency_signal_Hz#

Signal frequencies \(\nu_{s,i}\), needed to turn the ASE into a noise figure.

Type:

np.ndarray or float

loss_only_W#

The signal profiles the same fibre would give with the pumps off, \(P_{s,i}(0) e^{-\alpha_{s,i} z}\). Carrying them makes the on-off gain a ratio of two stored curves rather than a recomputation.

Type:

np.ndarray

Examples

>>> solution = solve_raman(length_km=80.0, gain_peak_W_km=0.4,
...                        pump_backward_W=0.2, signal_W=1e-3)
>>> round(solution.on_off_gain_dB, 2)
5.97

solve_raman(*, length_km: float, gain_peak_W_km: float, signal_W: ArrayLike = 0.001, pump_forward_W: ArrayLike = 0.0, pump_backward_W: ArrayLike = 0.0, alpha_signal_dB_km: ArrayLike = 0.2, alpha_pump_dB_km: ArrayLike = 0.25, wavelength_signal_nm: ArrayLike = 1550.0, wavelength_pump_nm: ArrayLike = 1455.0, spectrum: RamanGainSpectrum | None = None, bandwidth_Hz: float = 12500000000.0, temperature_K: float = 300.0, n_nodes: int = 401, tol: float = 1e-08) → RamanSolution[source]#

Solve the coupled Raman power equations along one span.

Signal Model#

Every wave – each signal channel, each pump, in each direction – is one power \(P_i(z)\) written in the \(+z\) coordinate, with a direction \(d_i = +1\) for a co-propagating wave and \(-1\) for a counter-propagating one. They obey

\[\frac{\mathrm{d} P_i}{\mathrm{d} z} = d_i \left[\sum_{j} C_{ij} P_j - \alpha_i \right] P_i\]

where the pairwise coupling is set by the gain shape at the shift separating the two waves,

\[C_{ij} = \frac{g_R}{A_\mathrm{eff}}\, \tilde{g}(\nu_j - \nu_i) \quad (\nu_j > \nu_i), \qquad C_{ji} = -\frac{\nu_j}{\nu_i} C_{ij}\]

A pair therefore always exchanges photons one for one, the energy difference going into a phonon, and that holds for every pair: pump to signal, pump to pump, and – the reason a WDM comb tilts – the blue channels of the comb to its own red ones.

Spontaneous emission adds, for each signal,

\[\frac{\mathrm{d} P_{\mathrm{ASE},i}}{\mathrm{d}z} = d_i \left[\left(\sum_j C_{ij} P_j - \alpha_i\right) P_{\mathrm{ASE},i} + 2 h \nu_i B \sum_{j:\, C_{ij} > 0} C_{ij} P_j \left(1 + \eta(\nu_j - \nu_i, T)\right)\right]\]

the last bracket being the phonon occupancy, which is why a Raman amplifier is quieter when it is cold. The ASE is amplified but does not itself deplete the pumps.

Boundary conditions. Co-propagating waves are known at the span input, counter-propagating ones at its output:

\[P_i(0) = P_i^\mathrm{in} \ (d_i = +1), \quad P_i(L) = P_i^\mathrm{in} \ (d_i = -1), \quad P_\mathrm{ASE}(0) = 0\]

With no counter-propagating pump everything is known at \(z=0\) and the system is an initial value problem, integrated with scipy.integrate.solve_ivp. Otherwise it is a genuine two-point boundary value problem and scipy.integrate.solve_bvp is used, started from the undepleted-pump profiles – a poor initial guess is the usual reason that solver fails to converge.

Undepleted limit. For one signal and one pump, when the signal takes a negligible part of the pump, the pump decays exponentially and the on-off gain has the closed form

\[G_\mathrm{on\text{-}off} = \exp\left(g P_p L_\mathrm{eff}\right), \qquad L_\mathrm{eff} = \frac{1 - e^{-\alpha_p L}}{\alpha_p}\]

which is what validation/optical_raman.py checks the solver against, along with photon-number conservation in the lossless limit.

Multi-wave solves. Every argument describing a signal accepts either a scalar – shared by the whole comb – or one value per channel, and likewise for the pumps; a scalar in gives a scalar out. Since a shift now separates every pair, spectrum= becomes required as soon as there is more than one signal or more than one pump wavelength: its default meaning, “the pair sits at the gain peak”, is only defensible for a single pair.

Axes: not a Processor – this is a power-domain solver, and it returns profiles rather than transforming a signal. See the module docstring.

>>> solution = solve_raman(length_km=80.0, gain_peak_W_km=0.4,
...                        pump_backward_W=0.2)

Three C-band channels under a single pump: the gain follows the shape of the spectrum, so the comb comes out tilted.

>>> comb = [1530.0, 1545.0, 1560.0]
>>> blow_wood = get_gain_spectrum("blow-wood")
>>> one = solve_raman(length_km=80.0, gain_peak_W_km=0.4,
...                   wavelength_signal_nm=comb, signal_W=1e-3,
...                   pump_backward_W=0.4, wavelength_pump_nm=1450.0,
...                   spectrum=blow_wood, bandwidth_Hz=0.0)
>>> print(np.round(one.on_off_gain_dB, 2))
[ 9.74 11.84 10.89]

The same total pump power split between two wavelengths halves the tilt – which is the whole reason multi-pump Raman exists:

>>> two = solve_raman(length_km=80.0, gain_peak_W_km=0.4,
...                   wavelength_signal_nm=comb, signal_W=1e-3,
...                   pump_backward_W=[0.15, 0.25],
...                   wavelength_pump_nm=[1425.0, 1455.0],
...                   spectrum=blow_wood, bandwidth_Hz=0.0)
>>> two.n_signals, two.n_pumps
(3, 2)
>>> print(round(one.tilt_dB, 2), round(two.tilt_dB, 2))
2.1 0.98
